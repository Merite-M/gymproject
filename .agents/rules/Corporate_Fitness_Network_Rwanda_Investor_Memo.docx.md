# **1\. Executive Summary**

The original idea was simple: companies often subsidize gym access for employees, but employees may not use a single assigned gym because it is too far away, inconvenient, crowded, or simply not their preferred facility. The proposed company would aggregate many gyms and fitness facilities into one corporate network so an employee can use the facility that is convenient to them, while the employer gets one managed benefit and providers gain incremental demand.

Our research materially strengthens the idea but also changes its definition. We should not build “another gym membership app.” We should build the B2B infrastructure that sits between employers and a fragmented fitness-provider market: provider aggregation, employee eligibility, access verification, usage accounting, billing, reporting, and eventually broader employee wellness.

| Our common agreement We should pursue this as a calculated venture opportunity, not as a hobby project. We will commit founder time only after the economics clear explicit gates: employers must pay, providers must accept our commercial terms, verified usage must generate positive contribution margin, and the model must demonstrate repeatability beyond founder relationships. |

# **2\. Evolution of the Idea: What Changed and Why**

It is important to preserve the original insight while documenting the changes made after research. This prevents “idea drift” and makes the investment thesis easier to defend.

| Stage | Original thinking | What we now believe |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1\. Initial observation | Companies pay gyms so employees can exercise. Some employees underuse the benefit. | Correct. Underutilization is a real economic and ROI problem worth investigating. |
| 2\. Network concept | Partner with gyms across a district/region and let employees use any nearby gym. | Still core. Network breadth and proximity are the employee-facing value proposition. |
| 3\. Revenue | Employer pays us; we route money to gyms and keep what remains. | Refined. We need a deliberately designed margin, not residual money. Revenue can be platform fees, per-visit margin, subscriptions, employee co-pay, and later upsells. |
| 4\. Payment mechanism | Maybe employers prepay gyms. | Uncertain. Public procurement evidence shows framework agreements and multi-provider fitness purchasing, but REG’s exact internal payment mechanics are not publicly verified. Treat per-use accounting as a hypothesis until field-validated. |
| 5\. App-first | Build the platform/app to manage the system. | Reversed. Prove employer/provider economics manually before spending heavily on technology. |
| 6\. “Gym marketplace” | Connect employees with gyms. | Too narrow. Position as corporate fitness infrastructure: access \+ verification \+ billing \+ reporting \+ network. |
| 7\. Long-term | Gym access. | Potentially employee wellness infrastructure: fitness, swimming, sports, nutrition, mental wellness, physiotherapy, screening, challenges, and wellness wallets. |
| 8\. Scale vision | Maybe a local business. | Rwanda is the proving ground; East Africa is the larger potential market if the model is repeatable. |

# **3\. The Problem We Are Solving**

The employer fitness benefit is fragmented. An employer may have one or several direct gym relationships. Employees may want different locations or services. HR/procurement/finance may have to manage multiple providers, attendance questions, invoices, substitutions, and employee eligibility.

| Stakeholder | Current pain | Value created by the network |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Employer / HR | Multiple providers, low visibility into utilization, administrative burden, uncertainty about benefit ROI. | One contract, one dashboard, one invoice, network-wide employee choice, utilization analytics. |
| Employee | Assigned facility may be inconvenient; benefit may not match work/home location or preferred activity. | Choice of nearby participating providers; simple access; optional upgrades. |
| Gym / fitness provider | Needs customers; may have unused capacity; corporate sales are fragmented. | Incremental corporate demand, centralized billing, predictable network channel, better utilization. |
| Finance / Procurement | Needs auditable usage, prices, contracts, and payment records. | Standardized invoices, verified transactions, provider reconciliation, audit trail. |

| Key behavioral hypothesis Convenience increases utilization. An employee who can use the facility near work on weekdays and near home on weekends should be more likely to use the benefit than an employee restricted to one facility. This is a hypothesis to measure, not a fact to assume. |
| :---- |

# **4\. Evidence That the Market Exists in Rwanda**

The strongest validation is not social-media interest. It is revealed willingness to pay by organizations already purchasing fitness and wellness services.

* Equity Bank Rwanda issued an April 2026 tender for gym and swimming-pool fitness services for staff, explicitly requiring facilities in Kigali, Musanze and Nyamagabe. This demonstrates employer demand spanning multiple geographies and facility types. (Source 1\)  
* World Vision Rwanda published a 2025 tender for wellness/fitness services for staff under a two-year framework agreement, renewable based on performance and funding. (Source 2\)  
* RISA published a 2025/2026 framework agreement for sports and recreational facilities and services for staff. (Source 3\)  
* Rwanda Airports Company advertised provision of sports and recreational facilities and services to staff in Kigali, Kamembe and Rubavu. (Source 4\)  
* Rwanda’s Ministry of Health launched a Workplace Wellness Program with the Ministry of Labour and Public Service, RSSB and PSF Rwanda, explicitly linking workplace wellness to productivity, healthcare costs, retention, physical exercise and preventive health. (Source 5\)  
* NISR reports roughly 4.8 million employed people in Rwanda in 2025\. This is not our addressable market, but it establishes a large underlying employment base from which a formal corporate-benefit segment can emerge. (Source 6\)

| Investor interpretation We are not creating demand from zero. We are trying to reorganize an existing spend category into a more scalable network and technology layer. |

# **5\. Market Definition and Initial Beachhead**

The total number of employed Rwandans is not our TAM. The initial addressable market is employers with enough formal structure and discretionary benefits budget to sponsor fitness/wellness services.

| Segment | Priority | Why |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Large private employers (100–5,000+ employees) | 1 | High employee density, HR functions, benefits budgets, repeated purchasing. |
| Banks / insurers / telecoms | 1 | High-value professional workforce; public evidence of fitness procurement in banking. |
| NGOs / development organizations | 1 | Workplace wellness procurement can be formalized and recurring. |
| Universities / large institutions | 2 | Large staff populations and recurring service frameworks. |
| Government entities | 2 | Real demand exists, but tender/procurement cycles and payment mechanics can slow early growth. |
| SMEs (20–100 employees) | 3 | Potentially easier to sell but smaller contract values and potentially higher CAC per employee. |

Kigali should be the main commercial beachhead because formal businesses and premium fitness supply are concentrated there. Musanze is strategically interesting as a smaller test market because the founder has a real-world connection to an employer already consuming the category, and recent public procurement evidence confirms fitness demand there.

# **9\. Unit Economics: The Number That Decides Whether This Is a Business**

The core unit should be a verified employee visit. The economics are deliberately modeled as hypotheses because actual negotiated provider rates and observed utilization have not yet been field-validated.

| Illustrative unit | Hypothesis |
| :---- | :---- |
| Employer revenue per visit | 4,000 RWF |
| Provider payout per visit | 2,800 RWF |
| Variable tech/payment/support cost | 300 RWF |
| Contribution per visit | 900 RWF |
| Contribution margin | 22.5% of visit revenue |

These figures are illustrative, not market facts or a forecast. The entire point of the pre-MVP field work is to replace them with contracted or observed numbers.

A second economic lens is implied price per visit from a monthly membership. Fitness Palace publicly lists 35,000 RWF/month for corporate groups of 50+ and a 5,000 RWF day pass. At 8 visits/month, a 35,000 RWF membership implies roughly 4,375 RWF per visit before considering any non-gym services, which helps frame a plausible test range for employer-funded network economics. This is a directional benchmark, not evidence that Fitness Palace would accept any particular wholesale rate. (Source 7\)

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1,000 | 35% | 6 | 2,100 | 8.4M | 5.88M | 0.63M | 1.89M |
| 10,000 | 40% | 7 | 28,000 | 112M | 78.4M | 7.0M | 26.6M |
| 50,000 | 45% | 8 | 180,000 | 720M | 504M | 36M | 180M |

Scenario math uses 4,000 RWF revenue/visit, 2,800 RWF provider payout, and 200 RWF variable cost for the scale cases above unless stated otherwise. It is intentionally simplified and excludes VAT, fixed payroll, CAC, bad debt, refunds, fraud, taxes, and working-capital financing. It is a sensitivity framework, not a forecast.

The key takeaway is not the absolute revenue number. It is the operating leverage: once a verified transaction network is built, additional visits can contribute margin without requiring a proportional increase in fixed overhead. Conversely, a small error in provider payout can destroy the model. Therefore provider pricing is the most important commercial variable.

# **12\. Provider / Gym Contract Model**

The supplier-side proposition is incremental corporate demand, not replacement of the gym’s existing customer base. We should present ourselves as a demand aggregator and payments/reconciliation channel.

* Standardize the commercial agreement across providers: reimbursement, eligible services, check-in method, operating hours, safety, dispute rules, payment terms, cancellation, fraud, and data handling.  
* Prefer variable reimbursement when the market supports it: the business should pay for verified usage rather than committing to full membership economics before utilization is known.  
* Create facility tiers: standard, premium, elite. Premium amenities such as swimming, sauna/steam, specialized classes, or high-priced clubs should carry higher reimbursement or employee co-pay.  
* Use geographic density as a product metric. “Enough gyms near employees” matters more than raw network count.  
* Protect working capital. Aim for employer payment in advance or short terms and provider settlement on equal or longer terms.

The Rwandan supply market is already heterogeneous. Fitness Palace publicly lists 50,000 RWF individual monthly, 35,000 RWF corporate 50+ monthly, and 5,000 RWF day passes. Platinum Gym Kigali publicly lists 120,000 RWF monthly individual and notes corporate/team rates are available. CSK offers gym, swimming, tennis, squash, basketball, football, sauna/steam and other facilities. This spread supports a tiered network rather than one flat reimbursement price. (Sources 7–9)

# **13\. Employer Contract Model**

* Employer buys access for a defined employee population.  
* Employer receives one monthly invoice and one utilization report.  
* Employer controls eligibility rules and benefit budget.  
* We manage provider network, access verification, reconciliation, and support.  
* Employee may optionally co-pay for premium access.  
* Employer contract should specify payment terms, eligibility, service scope, network changes, privacy, liability, fraud, reporting, and renewal.

# **20\. Rwanda Legal, Procurement and Tax Considerations**

This is a business thesis, not legal advice. Before signing scale contracts, the operating entity should use Rwandan legal/accounting professionals to structure contracts, tax treatment, privacy, liability, and public procurement compliance.

* Rwanda’s public procurement framework recognizes framework agreements, and recent procurement activity shows staff sports/wellness services being purchased through such arrangements. (Source 10 and Sources 2–4)  
* RRA states that corporate income tax is 28% from 2024\. (Source 11\)  
* RRA states that public-tender payments are subject to 3% withholding on invoice value excluding VAT, with additional VAT withholding rules for public procuring entities. This affects cash flow even when the tax is ultimately creditable. (Source 12\)  
* RDB describes business registration as online and free, so incorporation itself is not the main barrier. The commercial and operational system is the harder moat.  
* Because we may collect employer money and pay multiple providers, accounting treatment should be reviewed carefully: depending on contracts and control of service delivery, gross versus net revenue presentation can matter for financial reporting and investor optics.  
* Employee data and attendance records require careful privacy/security design. The scaled platform should minimize data collection, define lawful processing, restrict access, and avoid sharing unnecessary individual wellness information with employers.

# **22\. Founder Capital Allocation Philosophy**

Because the founder is a university student with other high-value career opportunities, this decision should be judged against opportunity cost, not just “can the startup work?” The correct question is whether evidence is strong enough that the expected return on founder time is superior to credible alternatives.

| Stage | Founder commitment | Capital philosophy | Go / no-go question |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Discovery | 10–20 hours/week | Very low cash spend. | Does the market reveal a painful, paid problem? |
| Pilot | 20–30 hours/week | Small controlled spend. | Can we make positive contribution margin on real transactions? |
| Validation | Full-time or near full-time only if gates pass. | Invest in MVP and sales. | Can we repeat employer acquisition and renewals? |
| Scale | Full-time founder \+ team | Meaningful capital. | Can network effects and economics support venture-scale growth? |

| Founder decision rule Do not sacrifice a strong academic/professional option merely because the idea feels exciting. Escalate commitment only when objective evidence increases the probability-adjusted return on your time. |

# **24\. Angel Investor Investment Thesis**

* Category validation: Rwandan employers are already procuring fitness and wellness services for employees.  
* Pain-point validation: multi-location, multi-provider purchasing creates administrative fragmentation and potentially underutilized benefits.  
* Economic logic: aggregate demand can negotiate provider economics and generate a transaction margin if usage is verified and pricing is disciplined.  
* Scalability: software and network effects can reduce marginal cost of adding employers/providers once the operating system is built.  
* Expansion: the product can evolve from fitness to broader wellness and from Rwanda to East Africa.  
* Capital discipline: the business can be tested with a manual-first pilot before substantial engineering or hiring.

# **28\. Bottom Line**

The opportunity passes the first investor screen: there is a real spending category, employers in Rwanda visibly buy fitness/wellness services, the supply market is fragmented, and there is a credible economic role for an intermediary that aggregates demand and simplifies administration. There are also local and global precedents demonstrating that network-based corporate wellness is a real business model.

However, we are not yet at “startup validated.” The critical gap is the unit economics. We do not yet know the negotiated provider reimbursement, real utilization distribution, employer willingness to switch from direct contracts, or payment timing. Those are not reasons to abandon the idea; they are exactly the questions a disciplined founder should answer before committing years of opportunity cost.

| Go-forward principle The next money and time should buy information, not infrastructure. Field validation comes before incorporation at scale, brand spend, or a custom app. Once the transaction is proven profitable and repeatable, the case for committing full-time becomes much stronger. |
